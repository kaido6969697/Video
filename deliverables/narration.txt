Someone rang the doorbell expecting a cash pickup. But the house looked like a funeral. The “dead man” was fictional. The grieving family were two YouTubers, waiting with questions. In that Los Angeles sting, the visitor was Jiandong Chen, later sentenced to six and a half years in federal prison. The twist is bigger than one awkward confrontation: prosecutors say the cash pickup was one visible piece of a fraud network that took more than twenty-seven million dollars from over two thousand older Americans.

So how did a refund turn into a box of cash?

Between 2021 and 2023, prosecutors say, victims—often in their seventies and eighties—received unsolicited calls, emails or computer pop-ups. The message might claim there was a problem with tech support, a bank account, a government issue, or a small retail charge. The goal was simple: get the person to call a number controlled by the scammers.

Then came the trust-building. Some victims were told they’d receive a refund. A screen appeared to show money sent back by mistake. The caller said: “You were overpaid. Send the extra back.” But the supposed refund was never real. The victims were being pushed to send their own money—by wire or, sometimes, cash in the mail.

That is where the story becomes a physical delivery system. According to the Justice Department, victims were given fake recipient names and addresses at retail locations that accepted express packages. Chen admitted using fake IDs matching those names to pick up packages of cash. Some money was collected in person. Prosecutors say the broader network also used remote desktop software to access computers, and cryptocurrency to move proceeds to overseas associates and pay co-conspirators.

The fake-funeral video captured one attempted pickup. Creators Ashton Bingham and Art Kulik, from Trilogy Media, confronted Chen at a home in the Los Angeles area. It was a dramatic moment—but not the whole case, and not proof by itself. The Justice Department says prosecutors also reviewed videos of Chen collecting packages and counting money. Their account traces his involvement from early 2023 through December of that year, including an attempted pickup in Upland after an earlier local arrest.

In April 2026, Chen pleaded guilty. On November 13, he was sentenced to 78 months—six and a half years. He admitted that losses during his role in the conspiracy exceeded nine and a half million dollars. Investigators identified more than two thousand victims and losses above twenty-seven million across the wider scheme.

That difference matters: the twenty-seven million describes the wider operation; the nine-and-a-half-million figure is tied to Chen’s admitted period of involvement. The judge said the harm was more than money—victims also lost trust, dignity and peace of mind.

The lesson is not “trust no one.” It’s: pause when someone creates urgency around money. If a company says you’ve been over-refunded, hang up and contact it using a number from its official website or your card. Never mail cash to fix a computer or return a surprise refund. Don’t give a caller remote access to your device. And tell someone you trust before moving a large amount.

A viral sting can make one face recognizable. But the evidence—victim accounts, records, investigators and court proceedings—shows the system behind it. The most important question isn’t how clever the scam sounded. It’s whether the target gets a chance to stop, verify, and ask for help.
